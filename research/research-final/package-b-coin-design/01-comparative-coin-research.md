# Comparative COIN Research

Status: design research for the 1984-1999 campaign. Binding decisions: [design-decisions.md](../project-management/design-decisions.md) (DD-01 to DD-15). Rule numbers refer to the local rules library `research/rules/md/`. History IDs (E-, F-, C-, M-) refer to `research-final/data/` and `research-final-claude/data/`. Every adaptation here is a design hypothesis. No game was played.

Inputs merged: Claude `comparative-coin-research/01-03`, Sol `01-coin-comparative-analysis.md` and `02-priority-design-questions.md`, `HandfulOfBandits/colonial-twilight-lessons.md` (CT lessons) and a rules extract of CT, BW Cyprus, ADP and PP.

## 1. What the COIN series offers this subject

The COIN series gives four tools that fit the Kurdish conflict in Turkey.

1. **Control is not consent.** Most volumes keep Control (pieces) apart from Support and Opposition (markers) (CT 1.6, 1.7; ADP 1.6, 1.7; AA 1.6, 1.7). The record needs this split. The State gained ground in 1993-1999 and lost legitimacy [F-049]. Compliance did not equal consent [F-034].
2. **Initiative is a cost.** A large action gives the next card to the opponent (CT 2.3.5). This prices big State operations without a combat table.
3. **Hidden organization.** Underground and Active guerrillas (CT 1.4.3) model a network that the State must find before it can remove it (CT 3.2.3, 3.2.4).
4. **Sanctuary as a rule system.** ADP Pakistan, CT Morocco and Tunisia and BW Thailand make an external refuge a restricted space with its own rules (ADP 1.4.2, 6.6.1; CT 1.3.4, 5.1.8).

The series does not give three things this subject needs [F-026; F-052; F-020; F-022]:

1. A host that turns hostile on its own interests. No host owns pieces or removes insurgent pieces.
2. A legal party that is banned and comes back under a new name. No volume has a ban and successor rule.
3. A national legitimacy cost that comes later than the action that caused it. CT Commitment is the nearest track. It does not carry a delayed charge.

Judgment (High): the series fits the subject as a mechanical frame. It is not evidence about Turkish or Kurdish history. Sol states this limit for every rule claim (Sol `01-coin-comparative-analysis.md`, "Scope and verification").

## 3. Colonial Twilight as the primary mechanical reference

CT is a two-player game with a full campaign of five Campaigns (CT 10.0). That matches DD-01 and DD-02. Its designer chose two players and moved the French Army, settlers and rival nationalists into events and systems (CT playbook p.12, per CT lessons). This supports the method of DD-02. It does not show that French and Turkish institutions were alike.

### 3.2 The largest change from both inputs

Sol drafted a ranked-choice initiative procedure. It ranked both players' choices and gave priority to the smaller action. CT gives next priority from the first player's choice only (CT 2.3.5). The Sol draft also said that passing loses initiative while its procedure could reward passing with priority (CT lessons, "The largest revision"). DD-04 replaces the Sol procedure with the CT pattern. Claude already used the CT track in its two-faction mode (Claude `faction-architectures/02-mod-2.md`, section 3a).

### 3.3 Where the analogy breaks

1. **Endpoint.** CT ends in a colonial exit. This conflict ends with territorial integrity, a changed PKK aim and continued conflict [F-037; F-061].
2. **Legal politics.** CT has no legal party. The evidence does not support treating legal parties as PKK organs [F-018; C-025].
3. **Government pieces.** The CT French and Algerian split must not become a Turkish and Kurdish split of State pieces. Guards are local and conditional [F-011; F-057].
4. **Hosts.** Syria, the KDP and the PUK changed on their own interests [F-020; F-022; F-051].
5. **Termination.** Talks and the capture of the leader do not give automatic victory or destroy the organization [F-021; F-038; E-0020].

## 4. Contributions from other volumes

### 4.1 People Power

PP is the nearest precedent for the MOD-3B module. It has a government, one armed insurgent and one nonviolent faction (PP 1.4). Four points transfer.

1. **Separate alignments.** Support (Government), Resistance (NPA) and Opposition (Reformers) are three markers (PP 1.6). A shift between two alignments passes through Neutral (PP 1.6.2). The Claude MOD-3B draft used one Opposition axis for both PKK and Legal Movement. The Claude R5 review rejected that (R5 D4). The module uses a separate marker (see 13-optional-modules.md).
2. **No Control for the nonviolent faction.** Reformers never Control. Their Bases count, and Activists count only with a Protest marker (PP 1.7).
3. **Three-faction eligibility.** The 3rd Eligible faction may take only a Limited Operation or Pass. Limited Operation and Pass keep a faction Eligible (PP 2.3.3-2.3.6).
4. **A small operations menu.** Recruit, Assemble, Persuade and Protest, plus Appeal, Convert and Canvass (PP 3.3, 4.3).

Limit: the PP Protest pool is shared with the NPA (PP 1.8.2). A Legal Movement pool with no rival is a design choice, not a precedent (R5 D6).

### 4.2 A Distant Plain

ADP gives the host posture model. The Islamabad track has five boxes from Hostility to Sponsorship. Hostility bars Taliban Rally in Pakistan and keeps Taliban there Active (ADP 6.6.1). Only Taliban stack in Pakistan (ADP 1.4.2). Air Strike may hit Pakistan and moves the track toward Sponsorship (ADP 4.2.3). DD-09 adopts the posture track and rejects the automatic Reset drift (ADP 6.6.1). A reset rule states a causal claim that the record does not support (Sol `01`, "Sanctuary"). The ADP playbook also explains why Pakistan stays a track: its interests do not fit a player seat. This is the test DD-02 applies to Legal Politics in C1 and C2.

ADP also shows two COIN factions with shared Control and separate scores (ADP 7.2). That is a precedent for MOD-4A, not proof that Turkey had two centers.

### 4.3 British Way

BW Cyprus gives a simpler two-player initiative. The leftmost chosen box decides next priority, and a Pass gives a faction-specific reward (BW Cyprus 2.3.2-2.3.5). It is the simplicity benchmark for the play aid. BW Malaya gives one shared Political Will track (Malaya 6.1.3). Sol rejected a single track for this design: it flattens survival, safety, representation and authority into one rate (Sol `01`, "Victory"). The BW designer notes say that civilians are abstracted and armed views dominate. That warns against claims that a harm track models civilian agency.

### 4.4 Fire in the Lake

FITL shows allied factions with shared Control and different scores (FITL 7.2). The US scores Support plus Available forces. ARVN scores Controlled Pop plus Patronage. The split itself is a historical interpretation (Sol `01`, "Why allied factions need independent success conditions"). FITL is the reference for MOD-4A only. Its Monsoon rule (FITL 2.3.9) is one model for a last-card restriction before each Propaganda round. DD-13 does not adopt it yet.

### 4.5 Gandhi

Gandhi gives three tools for a legal faction.

1. Nonviolent factions cannot Control a space but can deny Raj Control (GAN 1.7.1).
2. Arrested Activists go to a Jail box. They are off the map until released (GAN 1.3.9). The Raj loses 2 Resources for each piece in Jail (GAN 6.2.2). Detention has a price.
3. Nonviolent operations cost no Resources. A track caps the number of spaces: Restraint for Congress, Unity for the League (GAN 3.3).

The MOD-3B module uses the Jail pattern for detained Activists and the space cap pattern for Legal Space.

### 4.6 The Guerrilla Generation

The Guerrilla Generation (GMT, 2026) has a Peru game. The publisher describes emergency institutions, local self-defense forces and leadership vulnerability. All three are close to this design. Its final rulebook was not available in the local library or to the CT lessons review. Status: **unverified**. No mechanic in this package cites it. To verify: obtain the rulebook and compare its emergency, militia and leadership rules with DD-08, DD-09.6 and DD-12.

## 5. The three priority design questions

Sol framed three questions that change the faction list or the victory formulas (Sol `02-priority-design-questions.md`). Claude ranked ten questions by the same test (Claude `02-priority-design-questions.md`). The three Sol questions are the headings. Both inputs answered them for a 1993-1995 test. The answers below are for the full 1984-1999 campaign (DD-01).

### 5.1 Is the Turkish State one faction or several?

**Evidence.** On the PKK and on Syria the cabinet, the MGK and the General Staff acted as one. Open splits came on Iraq (Torumtay, Dec 1990) and Islamism (28 Feb 1997) [F-026; E-0174; F-060]. The MGK was a standing channel for military influence [F-027]. The Chief of the General Staff changed in Dec 1990, Aug 1994 and Aug 1998 [F-054]. A civilian-led policy shift in 1999 is not supported [F-061].

**Precedent.** ADP and FITL split the COIN side with shared Control (ADP 7.2; FITL 7.2). Those splits rest on foreign and host roles. No volume splits a state into civil and security centers in a domestic war.

**Sol test.** A separate seat needs a durable strategic aim, its own instruments and enough independent choices. Disagreement and autonomy alone do not pass that test. A score that rewards Security for continued war states a causal claim with no evidence behind it.

**Full-campaign answer.** One State faction (DD-03). Institutional friction comes from the Emergency Rule track, Legitimacy, event restrictions and Pivotal preconditions. The full campaign makes the split weaker, not stronger. In C1 a civilian seat would have few choices (Claude CSV note on MOD-3A). The open splits of C3 and C5 concern Iraq and Islamism, not the PKK [F-026; F-060]. The split stays as the MOD-4A experiment (13-optional-modules.md).

Judgment confidence: Moderate. Flip trigger: primary sources show durable, separate aims on the Kurdish question for civil and security centers.

### 5.2 How are local and legal Kurdish actors represented?

**Evidence.** Guards, tribes, legal parties and Hizbullah had opposed interests and cannot be one faction [F-057]. Guards were state-paid and gendarmerie-supervised. Recruitment mixed alliance, pay and coercion [F-010; F-011; E-0004]. Legal parties existed only from HEP, founded 7 Jun 1990 [E-0007; O-030; F-015]. Each closure produced a successor. The channel won local offices but no seats [F-052; F-016]. Party autonomy from the PKK is unresolved [F-018; C-025].

**Precedent.** PP and GAN give nonviolent factions that never Control (PP 1.7; GAN 1.7.1). ADP keeps a host with a weak seat as a track (ADP playbook, per Sol `01`).

**Inputs.** Claude preferred a Legal Movement player (MOD-3B). Sol preferred a non-player legal process with a three-player fallback from 1991. Both rejected a single "Kurdish coalition" seat and a Guards seat.

**Full-campaign answer.** Guards are local State pieces (DD-08). Hizbullah is event-driven [F-058]. Legal politics is a non-player Legal Politics system from C3 (DD-10). A Legal Movement seat would have no historical basis for about 38 percent of the campaign (Aug 1984 to Jun 1990). The non-player system also keeps C-025 open, because neither armed player owns it. The MOD-3B player module applies from C3 to C5 only.

Judgment confidence: Moderate. Flip trigger: C3 to C5 playtests show Legal Politics decisions are passive or owned by an armed player (DD-02).

### 5.3 What is the defining dilemma?

**Evidence.** Control and legitimacy diverged [F-049]. Coercion paid early and cost later [F-050]. Compliance did not show consent [F-034]. Sanctuary depended on host interests, not Kurdish solidarity [F-051; F-020; F-022].

**Sol thesis.** The power to suppress armed capacity and the power to make a lasting political settlement can diverge. "Control now, legitimacy later" is too universal as a rule. Some security steps can improve safety. Some repression silences opponents without visible mobilization.

**Full-campaign answer.** The dilemma is the same across all five Campaigns, but its tools change by Campaign. In C1 and C2 the State builds Guards and moves to OHAL [E-0004; E-0055]. In C3 and C4 the State can clear villages and ban parties, and each tool has a delayed cost [F-031; F-052]. In C5 the State can close Syria by pressure [E-0018]. Each player must meet at least two situations per Campaign where a short-term gain conflicts with a long-term goal. These situations must come from the action economy, not only from event text (Sol `02`). The structure is set by rules that switch on, not by a fixed snapshot (DD-01).

Judgment confidence: Moderate. Test: record per Campaign the count of player choices where the short-term and long-term options differ.
